# How to Start a Cleaning Business in 2026: Costs, Steps and First Clients

> **Disclosure:** This article contains a few affiliate links to software we recommend. If you sign up through them, we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.

**To start a cleaning business, pick residential or commercial, register the business in your state, get general liability insurance and a janitorial bond, buy basic supplies, set your prices, and find your first five clients through people you know and local listings.** Most new cleaning businesses cost between $2,000 and $10,000 to start. A solo house cleaner using their own car can get going for well under $2,000.

It's one of the cheapest businesses you can start. No storefront, no degree, and in most states no special license. That's also why there's a lot of competition, so the businesses that last are the ones that show up on time, price properly and keep their regulars.

This guide walks through it in order, with real numbers for each step.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - **Startup cost:** roughly $2,000 to $10,000 for most; under $2,000 for a lean solo start.
> - **Insurance:** general liability for a solo house cleaner runs around $44/month on average; a janitorial bond is often about $10/month.
> - **Licensing:** most states don't have a cleaning-specific license, but you still need to register and get a local business license.
> - **Pricing:** house cleaners commonly charge $33 to $75 per hour per cleaner, or $120 to $280 for a standard visit.
> - **First clients:** friends, neighbors, Nextdoor, Facebook groups and a Google Business Profile, before you pay for any ads.

## How Much Does It Cost to Start a Cleaning Business?

Here's a realistic budget for a solo residential cleaner who already has a car.

| Item | Typical cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Business registration (LLC or DBA) | $50–$300 | Varies a lot by state |
| Local business license | $50–$500/year | City or county |
| General liability insurance | ~$44/month (~$528/year) | Median for solo house cleaners |
| Janitorial bond | ~$10/month (~$120/year) | Often a $10,000 bond |
| Equipment (vacuum, mop system, caddies) | $200–$2,000 | A good vacuum is the big one |
| Starting supplies | $100–$500 | Then about $50–$300/month |
| Scheduling and invoicing software | $0–$49/month | Free plans exist |
| Simple website or Google Business Profile | $0–$20/month | Google profile is free |
| **Typical total to start** | **about $1,000–$4,000** | Not counting a vehicle |

Commercial cleaning (offices, gyms, medical buildings) costs more because you'll need commercial-grade equipment like floor machines, higher insurance limits and often staff from day one. Budgets above $15,000 aren't unusual there.

The biggest variable is transportation. If you need to buy or lease a vehicle, that one line can be larger than everything else combined.

### How to start a cleaning business with no money

"No money" really means "very little." You can't skip insurance, but you can skip almost everything else at first:

- Use your own car and a vacuum you already own
- Buy supplies job by job for the first few clients
- Use a free Google Business Profile instead of a website
- Use a free scheduling app (ServiceM8's free plan covers 30 jobs a month)
- Register as a sole proprietor or DBA, which is usually cheaper than an LLC

Some cleaners start by using their clients' supplies and equipment, which many homeowners are fine with. Ask when you book. Your first few paid jobs can then fund a proper kit.

## How to Start a Cleaning Business Step by Step

### 1. Choose residential or commercial

**Residential** (houses and apartments) is easier to start. You can work days, get clients by word of mouth, and recurring weekly or biweekly visits build steady income.

**Commercial** (offices, retail, clinics) pays bigger contracts and usually works evenings. But clients expect insurance certificates, sometimes bonding and references before they'll sign, so it's harder as a first business.

Most people start residential and add commercial later.

Within residential, you can narrow further: move-in and move-out cleaning, Airbnb turnovers, post-construction cleanup or deep cleans. A niche makes marketing easier because you know exactly who you're talking to.

### 2. Register the business

You'll typically need:

- **A business structure.** Sole proprietorship is the simplest. An LLC costs more but separates your personal assets from the business. The [SBA's guide to registering a business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/register-your-business) explains the options.
- **A business name**, registered with your state or county (a DBA if it's different from your own name).
- **An EIN** from the IRS if you'll hire staff or form an LLC. It's free on the [IRS website](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number).
- **A local business license** from your city or county.
- **A separate bank account**, so business money doesn't mix with personal.

Rules differ by state and city, so check with your local government or a small business development center before you start working.

### 3. Get insured and bonded

Don't clean a single house without this.

**General liability insurance** covers you if you break something or someone gets hurt because of your work. For a solo house cleaner, the median is around $44 a month according to insurance marketplace data, though quotes range from about $40 to $80 depending on your state and coverage.

**A janitorial bond** protects clients if something goes missing from their home. It's cheap, often around $10 a month for a $10,000 bond, and it's a real selling point. "Insured and bonded" on your flyer helps people trust a stranger with their house key.

**Workers' comp** becomes required in most states once you hire employees. It's usually the biggest insurance cost for a growing cleaning company.

Get two or three quotes. Prices vary more than you'd expect.

### 4. Buy equipment and supplies

Start with what you need, not a full professional kit:

- A reliable vacuum (this is worth spending on)
- Microfiber cloths, lots of them
- A mop system and buckets
- All-purpose cleaner, glass cleaner, bathroom cleaner, degreaser
- Scrub brushes, sponges, toilet brush
- A caddy to carry everything
- Gloves and basic protective gear

Buy supplies in bulk once you know which products you actually use. Many cleaners also offer a "green cleaning" option with non-toxic products, which some clients will pay extra for.

### 5. Set your prices

There are three common ways to price residential cleaning:

| Method | How it works | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Per hour | Charge for time per cleaner | $33–$75/hour per cleaner |
| Flat rate per visit | Fixed price based on home size | $120–$280 for a standard clean |
| Per square foot | Price scales with size | Common for move-out and commercial |

TaskRabbit's own data puts the average around [$33 an hour](https://www.taskrabbit.com/cost-guides/house-cleaning), while pricing guides from industry software companies quote $35 to $75 per cleaner per hour. Big metro areas often run 20% to 50% higher. Deep cleans and move-in or move-out cleans cost more, often $250 to $600.

Flat rates are usually better for you once you know how long jobs take. Customers like knowing the price upfront, and if you get faster, you keep the difference.

Before you set a price, work out your real cost per job: travel time, gas, supplies and insurance. Then check what three or four local competitors charge. Coming in a bit under is fine at the start. Being the cheapest in town usually isn't sustainable.

Say a standard clean takes you three hours plus 30 minutes of driving, and you charge $150. That's about $43 an hour of your time before supplies and gas. This is an example to show the math, not a promise of what you'll earn.

### 6. Find your first clients

The first five are the hardest. After that, referrals do a lot of the work.

- **People you know.** Tell friends, family and former coworkers. Offer a discount on the first clean in exchange for an honest review.
- **Google Business Profile.** Free, and it's how people find "house cleaning near me." Add photos and ask every happy client for a review.
- **Nextdoor and local Facebook groups.** Neighbors asking for cleaner recommendations is a daily post in most areas.
- **Realtors and property managers.** They need move-out cleans constantly.
- **Airbnb hosts.** Turnover cleaning is recurring and often booked weeks ahead.
- **Door hangers** in neighborhoods where you already clean. Neighbors see your car and your work.

Pay for ads only after these free channels are working and you know what a client is worth to you.

## How to Start a Cleaning Business From Home

Almost every cleaning business starts from home. You don't need an office, since the work happens at your clients' places.

A few things to check:

- **Zoning or home business rules.** Some cities require a home occupation permit, even if clients never visit.
- **Storage.** A garage shelf or closet for supplies is enough at first.
- **Taxes.** You may be able to deduct part of your home and vehicle costs. Talk to a tax professional, because the rules are specific.

## Mistakes to Avoid When You Start a Cleaning Business

- **Underpricing.** It's easy to win jobs by being cheapest and hard to raise prices on the same clients later.
- **Skipping insurance.** One broken TV or damaged floor can cost more than years of premiums.
- **No written agreement.** Write down what's included, your cancellation policy and how payment works.
- **Taking every job.** A one-off deep clean 40 minutes away can cost you more than it pays.
- **Mixing money.** Without a separate account, tax time gets painful fast.

## FAQ

### How much does it cost to start a cleaning business?

Most cleaning businesses cost between $2,000 and $10,000 to start. A solo residential cleaner with their own car can often start for $1,000 to $2,000. Commercial cleaning with equipment and staff can cost more than $15,000.

### Do I need a license to start a cleaning business?

Most states don't have a license specific to cleaning, but you'll usually need to register your business and get a general business license from your city or county. Check your local rules.

### How much should I charge for house cleaning?

Common rates are $33 to $75 per hour per cleaner, or $120 to $280 for a standard visit. Deep cleans and move-out cleans cost more. Check what local competitors charge before setting yours.

### Do I need insurance for a cleaning business?

Yes. General liability insurance and a janitorial bond are the basics. Many clients, especially commercial ones, will ask for proof before hiring you.

### Is a cleaning business profitable?

It can be, because startup costs are low and recurring clients bring steady work. Profit depends on your pricing, travel time, how many recurring clients you keep, and whether you hire. Plan conservatively for the first few months.

### Can I start a cleaning business with no experience?

Yes. Many people start by cleaning for friends and family first to learn how long jobs take and what products work. Practice on a few homes before you set your prices.
